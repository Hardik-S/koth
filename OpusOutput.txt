**Introduction**

Chess evaluation functions have long served as a benchmark problem in artificial intelligence, combining pattern recognition, strategic planning, and position assessment. King of the Hill (KotH), a popular chess variant on platforms like Lichess, introduces a fundamental alteration to traditional chess: players can achieve victory not only through checkmate but also by moving their king to one of four central squares (d4, e4, d5, e5). This additional win condition dramatically transforms strategic considerations, particularly regarding king safety and centralization.

In classical chess, the king typically remains sheltered during the opening and middlegame phases, with centralization occurring only in simplified endgames. King of the Hill inverts this principle, incentivizing aggressive king advances and creating unique tactical patterns where traditional evaluation heuristics may fail. Players must balance the conventional need for king safety against opportunities for central occupation, leading to complex non-linear trade-offs that challenge standard evaluation approaches.

This project examines whether neural networks can better capture these KotH-specific strategic patterns compared to traditional linear evaluation methods. The study focuses on early middlegame positions (plies 20-40), a critical phase where players transition from opening development to strategic middlegame planning. During this phase, decisions about king centralization become paramount, as premature advances risk tactical vulnerability while excessive caution may forfeit winning opportunities.

The research implements two evaluation approaches using supervised learning on historical game data. The first employs logistic regression as a baseline, representing traditional chess evaluation through linear combinations of handcrafted features. The second utilizes a feed-forward neural network capable of learning non-linear feature interactions. Both models share identical input features to ensure fair comparison, with differences arising solely from their capacity to model complex relationships. The methodology emphasizes maximum likelihood estimation through cross-entropy loss minimization, connecting theoretical foundations from the course to practical classification tasks in game evaluation.
